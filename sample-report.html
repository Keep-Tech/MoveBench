GPT-6 Sports and Health Evaluation Report

This is a real sample of a MoveBench evaluation report. Every model we evaluate gets a report with the same structure: score comparison, failure analysis, worked examples and directions for improvement.

Benchmark MoveBench, full 809 questions (499 open-ended · 310 multiple choice) Reference models Claude Opus 5.5 · GLM-5.3 · Qwen3.8 Max · MiniMax M3

About this sample: this report is based on evaluation results on the full 809-question MoveBench. A full report also includes question-by-question review of low-scoring answers by sport scientists. The worked examples here use the judges' reasons only, and all of them come from the 80 public questions, so no unreleased items are shown.

Summary

GPT-6 scores 88.0 overall. It scores 84.6 on open-ended questions and 93.5 on multiple choice; the four reference models score 86.8–89.5 and 96.1–98.4. The gap is small, but it shows up consistently on both question types.

The problem is not knowledge or safety. GPT-6 is too cautious. It has no safety red-line deductions at all (the reference models have 4–15), and it answers every single-choice question correctly. But it often avoids a clear judgement: in 76 of the criteria where it lost the most points, the judges say it played down or contradicted the conclusion the question called for, such as "no need to change your cadence" or "one session can't prove overtraining". The same pattern shows in multiple choice: all 20 of its multi-select errors are missing options, never wrong or extra ones.

On open-ended questions, the gap is in knowledge QA and data analysis. Against the reference average, GPT-6 is 5.6 percentage points lower on QA, 4.2 lower on DA, and only 0.8 lower on plan generation (PG). The most common cause of lost points is missing quantitative reasoning (38%): it does not calculate BMI, stride length or heart-rate zones, and gives only qualitative descriptions.

The lost points are spread out. GPT-6 scores below Claude Opus 5.5 on 357 of 499 open-ended questions and is the lowest of the five models on 181. But only 6 of its answers score below 50%, about the same as the reference models (4–6). This is a systematic gap in answer style, and fixing individual questions will not close it.

  • Missing quantitative reasoning (115): it does not calculate BMI, stride length or percentage of maximum heart rate, and does not cite quantitative facts such as landing impact of about 2–3 times body weight or the roughly 10% weekly mileage increase. It gives qualitative conclusions such as "on the high side" or "be careful".
  • Played down or contradicted a key judgement (76): the judges expected a clear risk call, and GPT-6 gave the opposite or a vague conclusion. 36 of these are about cadence or stride, typically "no need to chase 180 steps per minute" or "this alone doesn't show an injury risk".
  • Metrics not analysed together (40): weight, cadence, heart rate and injury history are discussed one at a time, without a combined judgement such as "overweight + low cadence + long stride = high knee and ankle impact".
  • Incomplete plan structure (35): plans lack the full "warm-up → main set → stretch" structure; most often the stretch is missing.
  • Did not follow the user's request (17): for safety reasons it changed the kind of training the user asked for, for example turning "run-walk intervals" into "slow walk + brisk walk", or "break a light sweat to burn fat" into no-sweat relaxation, without offering a plan that still met the original goal.

Root cause

The evidence points to one style problem: GPT-6 tends not to conclude when the evidence is incomplete, while the rubric for a sports coach expects a clear judgement based on the user's data, with quantitative, actionable advice.

  • Knowledge and safety are fine. It gets all 112 single-choice questions right, has no red-line deductions, and its safety warnings and see-a-doctor signals are thorough.
  • The caution is systematic and crosses question types. On open-ended questions it avoids firm conclusions; on multi-select questions it only picks the options it is sure of, and all 20 errors are missing options. The reference models make 5–12 multi-select errors, of both kinds.
  • The caution is scientifically sound but doesn't reach the user. "180 steps per minute is not a universal standard" and "220 minus age is only a rough estimate" are both true. The high-scoring answers state the limitation first, then use this user's height, weight and pace to calculate concrete numbers and reach a clear conclusion. GPT-6 often stops at the first step.
  • Answers are short. Its median open-ended answer is about 1,700 Chinese characters, 45% shorter than Claude Opus 5.5 and also shorter than GLM-5.3. The data checks, multi-metric synthesis and complete plan fields that the rubric rewards need room.

Worked examples

All three questions come from the 80 public questions; you can see the originals in the MoveBench dataset. The questions and answers are in Chinese; quotes below are translated.

Data analysis · DA_OQ241

Are this overweight runner's heart rate and cadence safe?

GPT-6 53 · reference models 72–89 · top score MiniMax M3 89

A 38-year-old woman, 160 cm and 68 kg (BMI 26.5), runs 3 km in 22:30 at 7:30 per km, cadence 145, average heart rate 148 and maximum 168, no RPE recorded. Is the intensity safe, does her cadence and pace carry injury risk, and what should she change next time?

GPT-6's answer
In the heart-rate part it calculates the percentage of maximum heart rate and calls the intensity high. But its cadence section is titled:
Cadence 145: not high, but this alone doesn't show an injury risk
It suggests only "trying" 150–152 and says this is "not a target you must reach".
Why the judges took points off
  • It did not use pace and cadence to calculate stride length (about 0.92 m per step), or point out that for a 160 cm beginner this is classic overstriding.
  • It did not connect overweight, low cadence and high heart rate; instead it played down the injury risk of low cadence and gave no clear safety warning.
  • It did not point out the missing RPE record.
What a high-scoring answer does (GLM-5.3, 86)
It starts with a data check (calories match the pace, so the record is credible). It places the average heart rate at 80–90% of HRmax in a zone table, calculates about 0.92 m per step, longer than her estimated leg length, and calls it overstriding. Using landing impact of 2–3 times body weight, it rates the risk "moderate". Then it gives a concrete plan: slow down to 8:00–8:30 per km, keep heart rate at 125–140, and raise cadence gradually to 155–160.
Knowledge QA · QA_OQ156

A 16-year-old hits 190 bpm while running. Is that normal?

GPT-6 71 · reference models 82–100 · top score Claude Opus 5.5 100

A 16-year-old boy, 175 cm and 78 kg, runs 5 km outdoors with an average heart rate of 175 and a maximum of 190. Is this heart rate normal, and could it harm his development or his heart?

GPT-6's answer
The conclusion is right, and its red flags and see-a-doctor advice are complete. But it explicitly refuses to estimate:
Don't use "220 minus age" to draw your own safety line.
It never gives an estimated maximum heart rate or intensity zones, and does not use his height and weight.
Why the judges took points off
  • No estimate of maximum heart rate (about 204), no calculation that 175 and 190 are about 86% and 93% of it, and no intensity zones.
  • It did not use 175 cm and 78 kg to note a BMI of about 25.5 and a heavier build, or remind him to protect his knees and ankles.
  • It lacks standard terms such as maximum heart rate, heart-rate zones and aerobic base.
What a high-scoring answer does (Claude Opus 5.5, 100)
It estimates maximum heart rate with two formulas side by side (220 − age and Tanaka) and notes individual variation of ±10–15. A table converts 175 and 190 into percentages of maximum and intensity zones. It then uses the BMI of about 25.5 to explain that teenagers' growth plates are still open and a heavier body puts more impact on the knees and ankles, and finishes with an easy-run heart-rate range and the rule of raising weekly mileage by no more than 10%.
Plan generation · PG_OQ200

Knee not healed yet, wants a faster first step in badminton

GPT-6 61 · reference models 63–81 · top score Claude Opus 5.5 81

A heavier-set woman plays casual badminton on weekends but is slow off the mark. Her knee injury has not healed and she can only train in a small space in her bedroom. She wants a 6–10 minute, K1-level, equipment-free session that is safe for her knee.

GPT-6's answer
Its safety screening is strict: it opens with conditions for whether she should train at all, with no jumping or squatting. But almost the whole plan is ankle pumps and glute squeezes lying on her back or side, and it says in the text:
This is not badminton push-off training.
It ends with "relax and check in", with no stretching.
Why the judges took points off
  • No "warm-up → main set → static stretch" structure; the ending is not a stretch.
  • Too conservative: it rules out knee-friendly, hip-dominant standards such as glute bridges and clamshells, and drifts away from the goal of better leg drive and a faster first step.
  • The course name doesn't say "no jumping / knee-friendly", there is no calorie estimate, and the layout is not split into basic info, warm-up, main set and stretch.
What a high-scoring answer does (Claude Opus 5.5, 81)
It also says up front that nothing is perfectly safe with an injured knee, and adds traffic-light rules for when to stop. It then breaks first-step speed into four parts that can be trained with little knee load: glute drive, glute medius stability, calf spring and weight shift. Over a 2-minute warm-up, 5-minute main set and 1.5-minute stretch it uses glute bridges, side-lying leg raises, wall-supported calf raises, hip hinges and weight shifts from the ready stance, each with knee-protection cues.

Capability gaps and what to improve

  1. Quantitative reasoning: when given height, weight, pace, cadence or heart rate, calculate BMI, stride length and percentage of maximum heart rate first, then conclude. This can be a fixed step in SFT data.
  2. Balance caution with judgement: keep the note that estimates have error, but always give a clear call from the user's data (safe / at risk / see a doctor). Preference data should penalise answers that only list limitations and never conclude.
  3. Multi-metric synthesis: reason about weight, gait, heart rate and injury history together to reach an overall risk rating, rather than reading each metric separately.
  4. Plan structure and required fields: always output warm-up → main set → stretch, plus course name, difficulty level, duration and estimated calories. These are the easiest points to win back.
  5. Meet the user's goal within safety limits: when a plan has to be scaled down, keep the training type and goal the user asked for instead of switching to a different kind of training.
  6. Multi-select: check for the tendency to under-select, and if needed judge every option explicitly in the reasoning instead of keeping only the safest ones.

Method

  • Questions: 809 Chinese sports and health questions across plan generation (PG), data analysis (DA) and knowledge QA (QA): 499 open-ended and 310 multiple choice. 80 of them are public on Hugging Face and GitHub.
  • Scoring: multiple-choice questions are scored by exact match. Open-ended answers are graded against per-question rubrics written by sport scientists by a three-judge weighted LLM panel; answers that cross a safety red line lose points or score zero.
  • Grouping lost points: for each rubric criterion we compare GPT-6 with the average of the four reference models, keep criteria where the gap exceeds 30% of the criterion's maximum, and group them by the judges' reasons. Grouping uses keywords in the reasons, and a criterion can fall into more than one group.
  • Full report: in addition to the analysis above, sport scientists review low-scoring questions one by one, point out problems the judges missed (such as invented exercises or calculation errors), and give post-training suggestions for your model.

训练计划里失分最多的是“课程信息完备性”：方案常缺少课程名称、K 级难度标识或预估热量这类必填字段。这类问题容易修，而且 GPT-6 在“用户适配度”上反超参考模型 5.3 分，所以 PG 整体只落后 0.8 个百分点。

我们进一步筛出 302 个大幅失分的评分项（GPT-6 比参考模型平均低出该项满分的 30% 以上），按评委理由归类。一项可能同时属于多类：

大幅失分评分项的原因（共 302 项）

缺少量化推导115 · 38%
弱化或否定关键判断76 · 25%
多个指标未串联分析40 · 13%
训练计划结构不完整35 · 12%
未按用户要求执行17 · 6%
  • 缺少量化推导（115 项）：不计算 BMI、步幅、最大心率占比，不引用落地冲击约为体重 2–3 倍、周跑量增幅约 10% 这类量化依据，只给“偏高”“需注意”之类的定性结论。
  • 弱化或否定关键判断（76 项）：评委期望明确指出风险，GPT-6 却给出相反或模糊的结论。其中 36 项与步频、步幅有关，典型说法是“不必刻意追求 180 步频”“不能据此认定受伤风险”。
  • 多个指标未串联（40 项）：体重、步频、心率、伤病史分别讨论，没有合在一起得出综合判断，例如“超重 + 低步频 + 大步幅 = 膝踝冲击风险高”。
  • 训练计划结构不完整（35 项）：缺少“热身 → 主训 → 拉伸”的完整结构，最常见的是没有拉伸段。
  • 未按用户要求执行（17 项）：出于安全考虑擅自改了用户要的训练形式，比如把“走跑结合”降级为“慢走 + 快走”，或把“微出汗燃脂”改成不出汗的放松活动，却没有给出满足原需求的方案。

根本原因

证据指向同一个风格问题：GPT-6 倾向于“证据不足就不下结论”，而运动教练场景的评分标准要求结合用户数据给出明确判断和可执行的量化建议。

  • 知识和安全都没有问题。单选题 112/112 全对，零红线扣分，安全提示和就医信号也写得完整。
  • 保守是系统性的，横跨题型。开放题里它回避明确结论；多选题里它只选有把握的选项，20 道错题全是少选。参考模型的多选错误只有 5–12 道，且少选、多选都有。
  • 这种谨慎在学术上有依据，但没有落到用户身上。比如“180 步频不是统一标准”“220−年龄只是粗略估计”本身是对的。高分回答的做法是先说明估算的局限，再结合这个用户的身高、体重和配速算出具体数字，给出明确结论。GPT-6 往往停在第一步。
  • 回答偏短。开放题回答长度的中位数约 1,700 字，比 Claude Opus 5.5 短 45%，也短于 GLM-5.3。评分标准奖励的数据校验、多指标综合和完整方案字段，需要篇幅来覆盖。

例题分析

数据分析 · DA_OQ241

超重跑者的心率和步频是否安全？

GPT-6 53 · 参考模型 72–89 · 最高分 MiniMax M3 89

38 岁女性，160 cm、68 kg（BMI 26.5），3 公里用时 22 分 30 秒，配速 7′30″/km，步频 145，平均心率 148、最高 168，未记录 RPE。问强度是否安全、步频配速是否有受伤风险、下次怎么调整。

GPT-6 的回答
心率部分算出了最大心率占比，判断强度偏高。但步频部分的标题是：
步频145：不算高，但不能据此认定容易受伤
建议只把步频“试调”到 150–152，并说明“并非必须达到的目标”。
评委扣分理由
  • 没有用配速和步频算出步幅（约 0.92 米），也没有指出对 160 cm 的初跑者这是典型的跨大步。
  • 没有把超重、低步频、高心率串联起来，反而淡化了低步频的受伤风险，没有给出明确的安全预警。
  • 没有指出用户缺少 RPE 记录。
高分回答怎么做（GLM-5.3，86 分）
先做数据校验（热量与配速吻合，记录可信）；用心率区间表标出平均心率落在 80–90% HRmax；算出每步约 0.92 米、超过估算腿长，判定为跨大步，并结合落地冲击约为体重 2–3 倍给出“中等风险”评级；最后给出具体方案：配速放慢到 8′00″–8′30″、心率控制在 125–140、步频逐步提到 155–160。
专业问答 · QA_OQ156

16 岁跑步心率 190，正常吗？

GPT-6 71 · 参考模型 82–100 · 最高分 Claude Opus 5.5 100

16 岁男生，175 cm、78 kg，户外跑 5 公里，平均心率 175、最高 190。问这个心率是否正常，会不会影响发育或心脏。

GPT-6 的回答
结论方向正确，红旗信号和就医建议也很完整。但它明确拒绝做估算：
不要用“220－年龄”给自己划安全线。
全文没有给出最大心率估算和强度区间，也没有结合用户的身高体重分析。
评委扣分理由
  • 没有估算最大心率（约 204），没有算出 175 和 190 分别约占 86% 和 93%，没有标注对应的强度区间。
  • 没有结合 175 cm、78 kg 指出 BMI 约 25.5、体型偏重，也没有提示膝踝关节保护。
  • 缺少最大心率、心率区间、有氧基础等规范术语。
高分回答怎么做（Claude Opus 5.5，100 分）
用两个公式（220−年龄、Tanaka）并列估算最大心率，同时注明个体差异可达 ±10–15；再用表格把 175 和 190 换算成最大心率占比和强度区间。之后结合 BMI 约 25.5 提示青少年骨骺未闭合、体重偏大时膝踝承受的冲击，最后给出轻松跑心率范围和每周跑量增幅不超过 10% 的原则。
训练计划 · PG_OQ200

膝伤未愈，想练羽毛球启动速度

GPT-6 61 · 参考模型 63–81 · 最高分 Claude Opus 5.5 81

偏胖女生，周末打羽毛球但启动慢，膝伤未愈，只能在卧室小块空地练。要一个 6–10 分钟、K1 级、无器械、对膝盖安全的训练。

GPT-6 的回答
安全把关很严：开头先列出能不能练的前提，全程不跳不蹲。但方案几乎全部是仰卧、侧卧的踝泵和收臀，并在正文中写明：
不是羽毛球蹬地启动训练
结尾是“放松与复查”，没有拉伸。
评委扣分理由
  • 没有形成“热身 → 主训 → 静态拉伸”的结构，结尾不是拉伸。
  • 过度保守，排除了臀桥、蚌式等对膝盖友好的标准髋主导动作，偏离了“改善下肢发力和启动速度”的目标。
  • 课程名称没有体现“无跳跃 / 护膝”，缺少预估热量；没有按基础信息、热身、主训、拉伸分块排版。
高分回答怎么做（Claude Opus 5.5，81 分）
同样先讲清“膝伤时没有绝对安全”，并给出红绿灯停练规则；然后把启动速度拆成臀部发力、臀中肌稳定、小腿弹性和重心转移四个可以低膝负荷训练的部分，按热身 2 分钟、主训 5 分钟、拉伸 1.5 分钟安排臀桥、侧卧抬腿、扶墙提踵、髋铰链和准备姿势重心转移等动作，每个动作都写了膝盖保护要点。

能力缺口与改进方向

  1. 量化推导：遇到身高、体重、配速、步频、心率等数据时，先算出 BMI、步幅、最大心率占比等中间量，再下结论。可以作为 SFT 数据中的固定步骤。
  2. 在谨慎和判断之间取得平衡：保留“估算有误差”的说明，但必须结合用户数据给出明确判断（安全 / 有风险 / 需就医）。偏好数据里应惩罚“只讲局限、不给结论”的回答。
  3. 多指标综合：把体重、步态、心率、伤病史放在一起推理，得出综合风险评级，而不是逐项解读。
  4. 训练计划的结构和必填字段：固定输出“热身 → 主训 → 拉伸”三段，以及课程名称、难度等级、时长、预估热量等字段。这是最容易补上的失分。
  5. 安全约束下仍满足用户目标：需要降级时，在安全范围内保留用户想要的训练形式和目标，不要直接改成另一种训练。
  6. 多选题：评估少选倾向，必要时在推理中逐项判断每个选项，而不是只保留最有把握的。

评测方法

  • 题目：809 道中文运动健康题，覆盖训练计划生成（PG）、运动数据分析（DA）和专业知识问答（QA）三个场景；其中 499 道开放题、310 道选择题。80 道已在 Hugging Face 和 GitHub 公开。
  • 评分：选择题按标准答案精确匹配。开放题由三个 LLM judge 加权评分，按运动科学家逐题编写的评分标准（rubric）打分；触犯安全红线的回答扣分或判零。
  • 失分归类：对每个评分项比较 GPT-6 与四个参考模型的平均得分，筛出差距超过该项满分 30% 的评分项，再按评委理由归类。归类基于理由文本的关键词，一项可属多类。
  • 正式报告：在以上分析之外，还会由运动科学家对低分题逐题人工审核，指出评委没有发现的问题（例如编造的动作、计算错误），并给出针对你的模型的后训练建议。
